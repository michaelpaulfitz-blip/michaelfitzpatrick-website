NEDWaur In design

The project portal for the NED's LAN-party game: a forest brawler with a Warlock lava arena at its heart. Updated 2026-10-15 · 117 ideas logged.

The concept

NEDWaur is a chaotic top-down brawler for the NED's LAN crew. Everyone roams a big forest, fighting mobs and leveling up, and every so often the whole server gets yanked into a Warlock-style shrinking lava arena to settle things.

Forest phase. A large forested map with WASD top-down control. You fight mobs for XP and gold, level up, and decide whether to pick fights with other players or avoid them and farm.

Arena phase. Triggers (a timer, a usable item, more to come) teleport players onto a shrinking platform over lava, exactly like Warlock. Formats range from free-for-all to 1v1, team fights, and one-vs-everyone with god powers. Small fights stay short because everyone else is watching.

The vibe. Crisp controls and a high skill ceiling, with builds that stack in chaotic, unpredictable ways. The audience is the clan first, so imbalance and chaos are features for now. If it lands, maybe Steam later (and that is when copyright gets sorted).

Spelled NEDWaur: a play on the clan name NED's plus Warlock. This page is unlisted, not private. Nothing sensitive goes here.

Design principles

Everything here is proposed until Mike signs off. Mike's own principles come first, then the ones suggested by research.

From Mike

Crisp, clean gameplayProposed

Controls respond instantly and every hit, push, and death reads clearly on screen. If a moment feels mushy, fix the feel before adding content.

Why: Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006) found that intuitive controls track with players' sense of competence, which predicts enjoyment. Game-feel work (Swink; Jonasson & Purho's 'Juice it or lose it') shows feedback is a big part of how good a game feels to play.
In NEDWaur: WASD movement with no input lag, hit-stop and a knockback arrow on every push, and lava damage that flashes the screen edge.
source 1 · source 2
Rules anyone can understandProposed

A new player at the LAN should get the goal and their character's basic kit within one round. Depth comes from how the rules interact, not from how many rules there are.

Why: Spelunky is praised for applying the same small rule set to everything, so its emergent results stay predictable. Balatro's creator said he added the poker theme mainly to make the game easier to learn.
In NEDWaur: Forest: kill creeps, get gold, level up. Arena: don't touch lava, push others into it. Each character gets a one-line pitch on the select screen.
source 1 · source 2
A high, rewarding skill ceiling (micro over autopilot)Proposed

Good play, meaning aim, dodging, positioning, timing, and push angles, should beat a better build piloted badly. Builds amplify skill; they don't replace it.

Why: SDT research ties enjoyment to felt competence. In Warlock-likes, knockback spells near lava already make positioning the core skill.
In NEDWaur: Skillshot pushes with travel time, dodge windows, and Mike's DDR brawler and mental-math wizard, which tie power directly to execution.
source 1 · source 2
Chaos and randomness are featuresProposed

Every session should produce at least a few 'did that just happen?!' moments. Random events, wild abilities, and physics weirdness are welcome.

Why: Reviews of Gang Beasts and Spelunky 2 credit unscripted, systemic chaos as the main source of laughter and replay value in party and roguelike games.
In NEDWaur: Global wobbly aim, the gravity character's wormholes, pinball-ball abilities, and random arena triggers.
source 1 · source 2
Builds compound in unpredictable ways (the Rounds rule)Proposed

Upgrades should multiply each other's effects, not just add to them, so two ordinary picks can combine into something absurd.

Why: The Steam page for Rounds advertises 11.2M+ powerup combinations from just 65+ cards. Vampire Survivors' evolutions and Isaac's item interactions are widely cited as the main reason players come back.
In NEDWaur: A 'bouncy' projectile card plus a 'pull on hit' card turns one spell into a ricocheting vacuum.
source 1 · source 2
Harvest what the crew loves about DeadlockProposed

Find the specific Deadlock mechanics the clan enjoys and adapt them where they fit, rather than copying the whole game.

Why: The game is built for a specific group, and the crew already knows and likes these systems, so borrowing them lowers onboarding cost. (Mike's note: in Deadlock, farming creeps pays more than fighting players.)
In NEDWaur: Creep farming that out-earns player kills in the forest phase. The detailed Deadlock list belongs to the MOBA research dossier.
Spectated formats must be quickProposed

Any arena format that leaves people watching (1v1, 2v2, 1-vs-all) should finish fast so nobody sits idle for long.

Why: Party-game makers (Gaming Couch) argue rounds shouldn't last much over a minute. Warlock-likes shrink the arena every 10-30 seconds to force an ending.
In NEDWaur: A 1v1 duel shrinks faster than a free-for-all, with a hard sudden-death cap.
source 1 · source 2
Necessary things should never feel like choresProposed

Creep farming, minion feeding, and puzzle-casting should be interesting decisions, not upkeep. If something is mandatory and boring, cut it or automate it.

Why: Sid Meier's 'a game is a series of interesting decisions'. Zagal et al. list grinding as a dark pattern that spends players' time against their interests.
In NEDWaur: The beastmaster's most basic upgrade both feeds and strengthens the beasts, so the tension only shows up on the juicy upgrades.
source 1 · source 2
Fun first, balance laterProposed

During clan prototyping, an imbalanced but hilarious mechanic stays in and a balanced but boring one goes out. Real balancing waits for a possible Steam release.

Why: Players remember a fun game's broken builds as stories. Rounds and Vampire Survivors spread partly through people sharing absurdly overpowered builds.
In NEDWaur: The steam-train character can be wildly strong in the lava arena. Playtest first, then nerf.

Characters built for the crew's interestsProposed

Theme characters around what specific clan members love (motorcycles, Company of Heroes, Alien) so players see themselves in the roster.

Why: Relatedness is one of the three needs that independently predicted enjoyment of multiplayer games in Ryan et al. (2006). Spectator research also finds subject matter drives engagement.
In NEDWaur: The motorcycle momentum character, the CoH squad blob, and the Xenomorph.

Suggested by research

Randomize the offer, not the outcomeProposed

Put randomness where players can react to it, such as which cards appear, which events trigger, and map layout. Keep the core combat result (did my push land?) deterministic. Any percentage procs should use pseudo-random distribution so streaks stay rare.

Why: Burgun argues output randomness hides why you won or lost and slows learning, while input randomness creates decisions. Dota's PRD, inherited from Warcraft III, keeps chance effects from streaking.
In NEDWaur: Upgrade picks are a random 3-card draft, but spell damage and knockback are fixed. A '25% chance to freeze' uses PRD.
source 1 · source 2
Upgrades change verbs, not just numbersProposed

At least half of all upgrades should change how something behaves (it bounces, splits, pulls, leaves lava) rather than giving +X%. Behavior changes are what compound into surprises.

Why: The Rounds cards people remember are behavior changes like Bouncy, Explosive Bullets, and Chase. Isaac's memorable items (Brimstone, Mom's Knife) change how you attack. A flat +10% never makes anyone laugh.
In NEDWaur: The 'Ricochet' card makes projectiles bounce off trees and arena edges; 'Magma Trail' makes your dash leave a short lava strip.
source 1 · source 2
Get synergies from shared rules, not hand-authored pairsProposed

Give abilities and upgrades a small set of tags and hooks (projectile, on-hit, on-push, on-kill, pull, terrain) and have every upgrade work through them. Interactions then emerge on their own, without writing N-squared special cases.

Why: Spelunky gets emergent but predictable chaos from applying the same rules to everything. Isaac players report frustration when items that look like they should combine don't.
In NEDWaur: 'On-push: explode' works on the wizard's spell, the train's ram, and the gravity well's pull, because they all fire the same on-push event.
source 1 · source 2
Chaos must be readableProposed

Show every player's picks on or near their character and in a spectator strip. Telegraph global events for 1-2 seconds. State stacking rules plainly and avoid hidden curves.

Why: Risk of Rain 2 players complain that items with similar wording stack in very different hidden ways. The Mario Kart critique is that catch-up nobody can see feels like cheating. Hokra's designer aimed for play that is clear to someone glancing across the room.
In NEDWaur: Card icons orbit each warlock in the arena. A klaxon and a screen tint warn of the 'wobbly aim' event before it hits.
source 1 · source 2 · source 3
Comebacks are visible and earnedProposed

Let the losing side catch up through choices and skill: losers pick an extra card and the leader carries a bounty. Don't use hidden rubber-banding or a blue shell that punishes the leader for leading.

Why: In Rounds the loser of each round picks a card, which reviewers say makes losing feel like an invitation, not a punishment. Riot's rule is that comebacks must be earned by beating someone stronger. The blue shell is the classic case of a comeback tool that wipes out good play.
In NEDWaur: The forest leader glows and carries a gold bounty. In arena best-of-N, the round loser drafts a card.
source 1 · source 2
Chaos may steal control briefly, never for longProposed

Effects that take away control (inverted controls, wobbly aim, mind-control, pinball) last a few seconds, are telegraphed, and are funny to watch. Beyond that, chaos stops being fun and starts feeling like a punishment.

Why: SDT ties enjoyment to autonomy as well as competence. Burgun warns that randomness the player can't respond to causes the same problems as pure luck.
In NEDWaur: Inverted controls from a wormhole last 3-5 seconds with a visible countdown ring. Mind-swap ends early if the swapped body takes lava damage.
source 1 · source 2
Spectators should have a stakeProposed

Anyone watching a duel should have something riding on it or some way to affect it, such as betting forest gold or voting on a hazard, so watching is part of the game.

Why: Spectator design work (Pirker, GDC 2019; UXPA) recommends making onlookers feel they win or lose along with the players. Hokra's designer found watching others play was as gripping as playing.
In NEDWaur: During a 1v1, everyone else bets gold on the winner. Eliminated players become ghosts who vote on the next shrink direction.
source 1 · source 2
Make the big moments landProposed

Lava kills, multi-kills, comebacks, and absurd combos get hit-stop, slow motion, a sound sting, and a callout, so the room reacts together.

Why: 'Juice it or lose it' and Vlambeer's screenshake talk show that feedback effects, not systems, are much of what makes a game feel alive. For a LAN, shared reactions are the payoff.
In NEDWaur: When someone is pushed into lava there's a 0.3 s slow-mo, a splash, and a kill-feed line naming the push combo.
source 1
Easy in, deep outProposed

Every character has a baseline where pressing the obvious button does something useful, plus a mastery layer (timing, angles, cancels, risk-reward) that experts can push. Execution-heavy kits like the DDR brawler need a forgiving floor.

Why: Gang Beasts shows that a low skill requirement lets mixed groups play together, but it's criticized for wearing thin. Sirlin-style competitive design wants a high ceiling. A LAN with 15 people of different skill levels needs both.
In NEDWaur: The mental-math wizard gets easy problems for normal casts and optional hard ones for crits. Missing a DDR note weakens the combo instead of cancelling it.
source 1 · source 2
Fun, not a casinoProposed

Variable rewards come from play inside a session (card drafts, creep drops, events), never from out-of-game currency, timers, or streak bonuses that punish you for stopping. A night ends cleanly at a natural stopping point.

Why: Zagal, Björk & Lewis define dark patterns as designs that work against players' interests, such as grinding, appointment play, and pay-to-skip. Vampire Survivors shows how strong gambling-derived hooks are, which is a reason to use them deliberately.
In NEDWaur: No login rewards and no persistent loot boxes. Unlocks, if they exist at all, are cosmetic or celebratory.

Engine and tech Proposed

The earlier Unity + Mirror pick still holds: host-authoritative, with a custom plain-C# physics sim. Start on Unity 6.3 LTS (6.0 LTS support ends Oct 2026), and replicate mobs, squads and minions as batched swarm streams, not one networked object per unit, which is the change the bigger scope forces.

First technical spike

  1. Create a Unity 6.3 LTS project with the latest Mirror; confirm NetworkDiscovery finds a host across two physical PCs on the LAN.
  2. Write the plain-C# sim core: fixed 30 Hz tick, circle bodies, Warlock DP knockback, one gravity-well force field.
  3. Spawn 600 dummy units: 400 creeps in 40 sleep/wake camps, 10 squads of 15 following a leader, 50 minions; pathing via budgeted NavMesh queries.
  4. Replicate two ways and record both: (a) naive NetworkIdentity + NetworkTransform per unit, (b) batched SwarmStream arrays with Hex Spatial Hashing interest.
  5. Load test with 15 headless bot clients (3-5 per spare PC) sending random WASD and casts; measure host sim ms/tick, host frame time while playing, bytes/s per client, total host upload.
  6. Add a 5,000-tree forest with a destroyed-tree bitset and the arena as a far-off region with a teleport-all trigger.
  7. Prototype body swap through the input-slot table, including a swap that is active when the arena teleport fires.
  8. Apply pass/fail gates: host sim < 8 ms/tick at 600 units, < 150 KB/s per client, host upload < 3 MB/s, 60 FPS on the slowest clan PC. If they fail, try Burst, then FishNet, before considering Netcode for Entities.
