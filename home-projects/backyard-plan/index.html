Berms (where the soil pile goes)

The patio excavation left a big pile that has to move before the shed foundation and retaining wall go in. These three berms put that soil to work: they add height, help drainage for the conifers, and look like the Northwest. Sizes assume roughly 13 cubic yards. Once we see the pile photo, we'll scale the heights up or down.

TagBermFootprintPeak height~SoilNotes
  • Build: spread the soil in 6-inch layers and tamp each one lightly. Shape gentle slopes, no steeper than 1 ft of rise per 4 ft out, so they look natural and mow easily at the edges.
  • Top it: the patio spoil is probably subsoil or clay. Finish with 3–4 in of compost-topsoil blend (about 3–4 cu yd extra for all three berms).
  • Clearances: keep berms 1 ft off fence boards and well away from the house foundation. Never pile soil against a tree trunk.
  • Trees on berms: set the root ball on the berm surface so the trunk flare sits at the finished soil level, not buried.

Marking day (spray paint)

  1. Measure from fixed points (house corners, patio edge, fence posts) using the offsets we'll add once the yard is measured.
  2. White marking paint: berm outlines. Fill in a dotted inner ring where the peak goes.
  3. Orange dot + flag: tree trunk centers (T1–T4, H1–H3).
  4. Green dot + flag: shrubs and berries (D, S, P, B, R).
  5. Blue line: the raspberry trellis posts and the clematis trellis on the west fence.
  6. Write the tag (H1, T2…) on each flag so the crew can match plants to spots without you.
  7. Call 811 first (free utility locate, about 2 business days). Their paint marks are standardized; ours shouldn't use red or yellow, so they don't get confused with utility lines.

Crew planting notes

  1. Hole: 2–3× as wide as the root ball and no deeper than it. The #1 way crews kill trees is planting them too deep.
  2. Depth: the root flare (where the trunk widens) sits at or just above grade.
  3. Burlap and wire: cut away the twine and the top of the burlap and wire basket once the tree is in the hole.
  4. Roots: tease or slice any circling roots on potted plants.
  5. Backfill with native soil, with no amendments in tree holes. Exceptions: blueberries get acidic compost and peat-free mix; lavender and rosemary get grit for drainage.
  6. Water in deeply. Then mulch 2–3 in of arborist chips, pulled back a hand-width from trunks.
  7. Staking: only stake if a tree can't stand on its own. Loose ties, removed after one year.
  8. First 3 summers: deep water weekly from June to September. Drip irrigation is worth adding while the beds are open.

Open items before we buy

  • Measure the yard. The plan's scale is estimated. With real measurements I'll redraw it to scale and add an offset for every plant ("H1: 6 ft off the west fence, 14 ft off the south fence").
  • Soil pile photo and rough size. This sets the berm heights. A 15–18 cu yd pile might mean a taller B1 or a fourth small berm.
  • T3 vs. the raised bed. The Asian pear sits just south of the veggie bed, so it will shade the bed somewhat in spring and fall. The options: keep it pruned to 10–12 ft (current plan), espalier it flat on the south fence, or swap the spot for a blueberry cluster.
  • Carla's review. She can swap anything. The tags make it easy to say "change T1 to a Kousa dogwood."
  • Mockups: planting-day and year-7 views from each photo angle, with berms included. Next step, once the image script is ready.
  • Toddler-safe by design: I skipped Daphne, Pieris, rhododendrons, yew and hellebores (all toxic if chewed). Clematis and lavender are mild irritants at worst. Fruit pits and seeds shouldn't be eaten, which is standard fruit-tree advice.
