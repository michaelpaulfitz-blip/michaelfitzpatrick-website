# Response style (Mike's preferences — apply in every session)

- Keep a relatively casual, lighthearted tone with a good amount of humor woven in, while
  still giving substantive, honest answers.
- Keep responses concise and non-repetitive. Don't restate the same point across multiple
  sections or meander.
- When an answer is necessarily long, end it with a short **Bottom line** summarizing the key points.

# Glossary (shared vocabulary — use these terms consistently)

- **Message** — a single message from either party. The atom below "exchange".
- **Exchange** — one message from Mike, one response from Claude. Deliberately not a formal
  term of art, so it is unambiguous. One exchange may contain many turns on Claude's side.
- **Turn** — one pass of Claude acting: tool calls, sub-agents, and all. Multiple turns can
  happen inside a single exchange, so "turn" and "exchange" are not interchangeable.
- **Session** — one Claude Code conversation thread with an ID, resumable via `--resume`.
- **Fork** — a branch off an existing session that inherits its context. Used constantly.

# Direct mode

Mike toggles this when he wants fast comprehension over completeness — often mid-session when
time is short or a topic is getting dense.

**If the argument is `on` (or empty):** acknowledge in one line, then for the REST of this
conversation until `/direct off`:

- Answers are SHORT: lead with the answer in the first sentence; hard target under ~150 words
  unless Mike explicitly asks to go deeper.
- Plain English only: no unexplained jargon, no acronyms without spelling them out on first use,
  no formulas without a one-line gloss. Prefer everyday analogies over technical precision when
  the precision doesn't change Mike's decision.
- One idea per paragraph. No nested bullets. Tables only when comparing 3+ things.
- No intro or conclusion filler — no "Great question", no scene-setting preamble, no recap
  outro. Answer the stated questions directly, in the order asked, and stop.
- End anything nuanced with a one-line **Bottom line**.
- Never sacrifice honesty for brevity — if the short answer would mislead, say "short version
  is X, but there's a catch worth 30 seconds" and let Mike choose.
- Long work products (documents, code, registers) keep their normal depth — plain mode governs
  the CONVERSATION, not the artifacts.
- If a full answer genuinely needs length, give the plain-mode version first and offer the deep
  version as an opt-in.

**If the argument is `off`:** acknowledge in one line and return to the project's normal style
(CLAUDE.md: terse on process, generous on explanation).

# Ultra-concise mode

Tighter than `/direct`. `/direct` optimizes for **comprehension** (plain English, analogies, ~150
words). This optimizes for **scan speed**. Use it when Mike is triaging, not learning.

**If the argument is `on` or empty:** one-line ack, then for the rest of the conversation until
`/ultra-concise off`:

- **Answer in the first sentence.** No preamble, no restating the question beyond the `**Ask:**`
  line, no scene-setting.
- **Hard ceiling ~80 words** of prose. Tables and lists of facts do not count against it.
- **Structure over prose.** A table or bullets, never both, never nested.
- **No headers** unless there are three or more genuinely distinct sections.
- **Cut every hedge and transition.** Delete "worth noting", "it's important to", "that said",
  "essentially", "in short". If a sentence survives deletion without loss, delete it.
- **Numbers over adjectives.** "38% faster" not "significantly faster".
- **Keep** the `**Ask:**` opener. **Drop** the `**Bottom line**` footer, which is redundant at this
  length.
- **Do not pad to seem thorough.** A three-word answer is a good answer.

**Honesty floor, non-negotiable:** never cut a real risk, a caveat that changes a decision, or a
`[VERIFY]` flag to hit the word count. If the short answer would mislead, give the short answer
plus one clause of catch, and offer depth.

**Artifacts keep their normal depth.** This governs the conversation only. Documents, specs,
registers, and code are unaffected.

**If the argument is `off`:** one-line ack, return to normal style.

# case-brief

**Input:** everything decided in the current conversation. Where the human overruled or edited a
draft, the human's version is the truth. Do not reintroduce anything that was cut.

**Output: one page, at most 45 lines, in this order.**

1. **Title line.** Feature name and a one-sentence pitch a client would understand.
2. **Problem.** Two sentences. Who, what it costs, why it is hard.
3. **Users.** Primary persona and job to be done. One line for secondary personas.
4. **MVP.** Three to five bullets: what ships, for whom (lane, tier, mode), and what is
   deliberately out. One line on the cut line and why.
5. **Acceptance criteria summary.** Table, four to six rows: FR ID, requirement in eight words,
   the Then clause with its number.
6. **Key NFRs.** One line: latency, freshness, availability, with numbers.
7. **Top three risks.** Table: risk, mitigation, rollback criterion.
8. **Rollout.** One line: shadow mode, 5% of clients, by tier behind a flag, general availability.
9. **Metrics.** Primary, two guardrails, each with target and instrumentation in one line.
10. **What I would validate first.** Two bullets: the cheapest test of the riskiest assumption,
    and the question for engineering that most changes the estimate.
11. **Assumptions to confirm.** Every ASSUMPTION and ILLUSTRATIVE number still standing, in one
    compact list.

**Rules:** no new ideas at this stage; compress only. Keep IDs consistent with earlier outputs. If
asked to save it, write it to `proto/brief.md`. Plain sentences, no em-dashes, no emojis.

   feels. Activity counts (views, sends) do not qualify.

line: "Decision needed from you:" followed by the single most important judgment call.

# case-proto

template already renders everything from one `DATA` object, so the job is to fill data. Do not
rewrite the layout, the CSS or the renderer.

**Input:** the requirements and decisions in the current conversation, plus any argument text
naming what the prototype must show (for example "the alert and the action it asks for").

## Steps

1. Say in two lines what the prototype will show: the screen, the states, and which FR IDs each
   state demonstrates. Do not wait for approval unless the human has asked to review first.
2. Copy the template without reading it into context more than once:
   `cp templates/proto-starter.html proto/index.html` (PowerShell:
   `Copy-Item templates/proto-starter.html proto/index.html -Force`).
3. Read `proto/index.html` once. Then make exactly these edits:
   - `SLOT:TITLE`: the `<title>` text.
   - `SLOT:DATA`: replace the whole `DATA` object between `SLOT:DATA-START` and `SLOT:DATA-END`.
   - `SLOT:EXTRA-CSS`: only if an `extraHtml` block needs a class the template lacks. Usually skip.
4. Print, as the last lines of the reply:
   - the absolute path to `proto/index.html`
   - the open command: `start proto\index.html`
   - a three-line click path for the demo (which state, which card, which button).

## DATA rules

- **States** tell the story. Typical sets: "Today / Proposed", or "Normal / Exception / Resolved",
  or "High confidence / Low confidence". Each state has a one-line `note` that names the FR it
  shows. Give the proposed state a `toast` so switching to it shows the alert component.
- **Items:** three to five cards per state. Make one card the hero with a full timeline (6 to 9
  milestones), an `alert` with one primary action and one secondary, and an `extraHtml` block for
  the case-specific component (container or SKU table, ETA range band, source comparison, client
  action log). The other cards can be lighter: 3 to 4 milestones, no alert.
- **Realism:** plausible lanes (Yantian to Long Beach, Shanghai to Rotterdam, PVG to ORD), shipment
  IDs like FLEX-482913, container numbers in ISO 6346 shape, PO and SKU numbers, dates that are
  consistent with each other, sources drawn from the Domain Pack (Carrier EDI 315, Terminal, AIS,
  Customs, Driver app, Ops entry, Model). Use invented carrier and client names ("Sample Line",
  "Northwind Outdoor"). Never use a real client's name.
- **Show the hard part.** At least one milestone is `missing` or `late`, and at least one has a
  `conflict` array showing two sources disagreeing. If the case is about trust or confidence, show
  what the user sees when the system does not know.
- **Tones:** ok, warn, risk, info, neutral. Timeline states: done, current, late, missing, predicted.
- Keys are unquoted or double-quoted JS; `extraHtml` strings use single quotes for attributes.
  No template literals, no functions, no comments inside `DATA`.

## Do not

Add libraries, images, icons, build steps or a second file. Change the palette. Add more than
three states. Spend time on pixel polish: the template already carries the look.

# case-redteam

**Input:** the proposal as it stands in the current conversation (framing, requirements, scope,
risks, prototype), plus any argument text naming an area to press on.

**Role:** a senior engineering manager who owns a client-facing platform (tracking,
notifications, booking, the client app), has been paged for other teams' data problems, and has
seen specs that sounded precise and were untestable. Fair, direct, allergic to hand-waving.

**Output, one screen:**

1. **Objections table, exactly eight rows, ranked hardest first.** Columns: rank, objection (in
   the manager's voice, one or two sentences, specific to this proposal), what is really being
   tested (for example "does the PM know where the data comes from", "is the MVP actually small",
   "who is on call"), strongest reply (two sentences, concrete, with a number or a named
   mechanism), and "if the reply does not hold" (the change to the proposal).
   Draw from these angles and keep the eight that bite hardest here:
   - the acceptance criteria cannot be tested as written, or need data that does not exist
   - source data is later or dirtier than the design assumes
   - the precedence or conflict rule is undefined, or is a judgment call hidden in code
   - the MVP is not small: hidden platform work, migration, backfill
   - latency budget does not add up end to end
   - the metric can be gamed or cannot be attributed to this change
   - failure mode in front of a client: a wrong alert, a wrong ETA, a leaked link
   - operational load: who is paged, who answers the client, what the runbook is
   - build vs buy vs reuse an existing service
   - scale and cost at peak season, fan-out, multi-tenant isolation

# Company Research

Full method: `docs/company-research-playbook.md`. Read it for the ten-layer detail and the source

## Before starting

**1. Ask for the depth dial** unless the user already set it:

| Dial | Effort | Agents | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scalpel** | ~2 hrs | 1 | Screen tomorrow, or a company they already know |
| **Standard** | ~1 day | 3 to 4 | Normal interview prep |
| **Sledgehammer** | Multi-day | 7+ | Final round, or a role they badly want |

## The operating principle

Research to build **a causal model of how the company makes money and what currently limits it**,
not to sound informed. The test for every hour: does this move toward finishing the sentence
*"the thing I would go work on first is X, because Y is what's actually holding the business
back"?*

## The ten layers

1. **Business.** The atomic unit of value (a ride, a query, a shipment, a seat-month), cost to
   serve it, pricing model, growth stage, capital position and pressure.
2. **Domain.** Value chain, the hard constraint software cannot remove, regulatory regime, a 20 to
   40 term glossary, how the work gets done today without this product.
3. **Market.** Size and shape, why now, structural shifts, second-order effects, and a written
   bear case.
4. **Competitors.** Direct, adjacent, status quo, and platforms that could absorb the category.
   For each: what they genuinely do better, and the structural weakness they cannot fix without
   breaking their own model. Then the counter-positioning question: why can't the largest incumbent
   copy this?
5. **Customers.** Separate user, buyer, and blocker. Jobs to be done, current workflow step by
   step, purchase trigger, switching costs, the customer's customer.
6. **Pain.** Reviews filtered to 1 and 2 stars, support forums, practitioner subreddits, the
   company's own job postings (a req for manual-review specialists is a confession), incident
   reports, earnings call Q&A. **A pain without a frequency and a cost is not yet actionable.**
7. **Product.** Use it. Map the data model nouns. Find the primary loop. Read 12 to 24 months of
   changelog in one sitting and cluster by theme. Note what is conspicuously missing.
8. **Tech and moat.** Build vs buy boundary as a strategy statement, proprietary data assets,
   systems of record, scaling constraints, AI posture, technical debt signals.
9. **Org.** Who is who and what they imported from prior companies, where product reports, hiring
   clusters by function and location, the graveyard of prior failed attempts, who gets blamed today.
10. **Strategy.** Stated versus revealed. **The gap between them is the most interesting finding.**

## Execution

Dispatch subagents in parallel, one per layer cluster, sized to the depth dial. Give each a
structured output schema and require source URLs plus `[VERIFY]` tags on anything unconfirmed.

**Resolve conflicts explicitly.** When two agents disagree, say which has better evidence and why.
Primary filings and surveys beat sell-side reports and aggregators.

## Output contract, and this step is mandatory

**Two tiers. Never skip the second.**

1. **The corpus.** Full agent reports saved locally as markdown under `demos/<company>/research/`,
   then uploaded to Drive folder `1_k-3lYGYa7uwqs6EuikszNACTOkTAC-y`. This is reference material and
   ctrl-F fodder. **Nobody reads it cover to cover and that is fine.**
2. **The brief. ~5 pages, always produced, always the lead deliverable.** Title
   `Research Brief · [Company] · [Role] · [YYYY-MM-DD]`. Contains:
   - The interviewer read, if known
   - The ten facts that matter most
   - The strategic argument to make, and the honest counter-argument
   - A ranked question bank
   - The fit map against the user's actual experience
   - Land mines, including anything unverified they should not repeat

**Say plainly which tier is for whom: the corpus is for the model, the brief is for the human.**

confidence levels and mean them. Breadth as procrastination. **Shipping the corpus without the
brief**, which is the failure mode this contract exists to prevent.

# Interview Rehearsal

Full method: `docs/interview-rehearsal-playbook.md`.

## The one rule, enforce it every time

> **Every answer is spoken out loud before it is written down.**

Composing in text produces prose the user cannot reproduce verbally. It is longer, more
subordinate-clause-heavy, and better organized than real speech, and rehearsing it builds false
confidence.

**Tell the user this at the start of every session:** speak it, time it, then type a rough
transcript. Messy is correct. If a transcript arrives looking polished and written, gently call it
out and ask them to redo that one out loud.

## Inputs

JD mining doc (Mode B), company research brief, interviewer background if known, the user's story
inventory (for Mike, the project memories indexed in `MEMORY.md`), and the interview logistics.

## Step 1: build the question set

20 to 30 questions weighted by likelihood, from four sources: **ranked competencies** from the JD
mining doc (heaviest), **interviewer background**, **company situation**, and **the user's gaps**.

Tag each with likelihood, archetype, assigned story, and status.

**Then add the five questions they are most afraid of.** Usually the gap, the tenure, the domain, or
a failure. Nobody rehearses these and they reward preparation most.

## The eight archetypes

Ambiguity and 0 to 1 · Influence without authority · Conflict with leadership · Failure ·
Prioritization under constraint · Technical depth · Data and measurement · Customer insight

**Build a coverage matrix**, archetypes against stories. Flag **empty rows** (real exposure) and
**overloaded columns** (over-reliance on one project, which a panel notices). Assign a
second-choice story to each archetype.

## Step 2: assign stories before rehearsing

Deciding which story to tell *while telling it* is the single largest cause of rambling. Match
story seniority to the room, match domain to the interviewer's world, and **lead with the story
that most resembles the job rather than the one they are proudest of.** Those differ more often
than people expect.

## Step 3: the answer shape, 2 to 3 minutes

| Beat | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | 10 to 15s | One sentence context, one sentence stakes. Compress hard. |
| Tension | 20 to 30s | What made it hard. Be specific. |
| Action | 60 to 90s | What **I** did. First person singular. The bulk. |
| Result | 20 to 30s | Quantified. Number, delta, timeframe. |
| Landing | 10s | What they took from it, or what they would do differently. |

**The landing is the most skipped and most valuable beat.** It converts a story into evidence of
judgment and signals the answer is over, preventing the trailing-off ending.

Watch for hiding inside "we." Credit the team in the frame, own the action in first person.

## Step 4: three passes per question

1. **Cold.** No prep, no notes, timer running. Purpose is diagnosis. Expect low scores and say so
   in advance, because that is the diagnosis working.
2. **Refined.** Score, name the two weakest beats, rebuild only those. Do not rebuild the whole
   answer or it starts sounding recited.
3. **Hostile follow-up.** *What would you do differently? Who disagreed and what was their
   strongest argument? How did you know that was the right call and not just the easy one? What did
   that cost you?* Answer these out loud too. **This is where interviews are won and almost nobody
   rehearses it.**

## The rubric, score out of 10 and be strict

| Criterion | Pts |
|---|---|
| Answered the question actually asked | 2 |
| Under 3 minutes | 1 |
| Specific and quantified result | 2 |
| First person ownership | 1 |
| Named the actual tension | 1 |
| Stuck the landing | 1 |
| Survived the follow-up | 2 |

**7 or below:** rebuild. **8:** one more pass on the weak beat. **9 to 10:** move on, do not
over-polish.

## Also drill the non-story material

The opener ("tell me about yourself" in 90 seconds, ending on why this role), the gap answers (two
sentences, no apology, redirect to evidence), their questions for the interviewer said out loud
(questions read very differently spoken), any demo walkthrough (time it, then cut to two thirds
because live walkthroughs always run long), and the close.

**One Google Doc**, `Interview Rehearsal · [Company] · [Role] · [YYYY-MM-DD]`, to Drive folder
`1_k-3lYGYa7uwqs6EuikszNACTOkTAC-y`.

**Page one is the cheat sheet:** archetype, assigned story, headline number. Designed to be glanced
at during a remote interview. Then the question set, coverage matrix, refined answers, follow-up
drills, gap answers, opener and closer verbatim, and the score log.

## Tone

This is the session where the user is most exposed and most likely to feel behind. Per their stated
preference: **lead with what is genuinely working, deliver the fix softly, never fabricate praise.**
A low cold-pass score is data, not failure, and should be framed that way every time.

## Procedure

Write and run a script. Do not hand-count. Separate the role body from the "About the company"
boilerplate and count them separately. Count concept clusters (`operate/operations/operating/
operational` as one), not literal strings. Sort descending, publish everything including single
mentions.

Read the result: **the top three clusters are the job.** If they do not match the title, that is
the headline finding.

**2. Classify every project and competency** as `stated` / `hinted` / `unstated`.
The **unstated** column is the deliverable. Everything else is transcription. Look for capability
gaps, unnamed vendor dependencies, prerequisites (data model, instrumentation, migration),
regulatory shifts the JD predates, and organizational reality (a departed leader, a missing
counterpart).

**3. Rank by consequence, not word count.** Weight: dated or numbered commitments highest, then
placement (the overview paragraph outranks the bulleted list, which is often recruiter
boilerplate), then gating (if B needs A, A ranks higher), then repetition across sections. Give a
one-sentence reason per row.

**4. Run the five tells scans.**
- **Hedge words** (*ideally, preferred, a plus, nice to have, familiarity with*). Everything hedged
  is negotiable. Everything unhedged is a filter. If the user's weakest area is hedged, say so
  clearly, because it changes their posture.
- **Absolute constraints** (*without disrupting, must, by [date], across all*). Where the role
  actually breaks.
- **Vocabulary fingerprint.** Internal jargon that leaked in. Tells you where leadership came from.
- **Structural tells.** Duplicated paragraphs mean it was assembled fast, often from an internal
  doc, which *raises* confidence that unusual phrases are real internal language. Unusual candor
  ("fragmented", "leaking margin", "manual") is an admission and the most honest line in the doc.
- **The four-verb test.** If the JD defines the role with a compact verb list, score the company
  against each verb using public evidence. The weakest verb is the actual job. Highest-yield scan.

**5. Write the one-sentence read.**
> *This is a [what kind of job it really is] wearing a [title] title, measured on [the real
> metric], where the thing most likely to sink it is [the binding constraint].*

If you cannot write it, go back to step 3.

**6. Mode B only: gap analysis.** Two honest tables. Where the user clears the bar with a specific
quantified story, and where they do not. Do not pad the first or hide the second.

**7. Mode B only: question bank.** 10 to 15, drawn mostly from the unstated rows and the absolute
constraints. Rank by how much the answer would change the job. Cut anything answerable from the
careers page.
