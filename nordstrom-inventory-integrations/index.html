How I built this, and how much to trust it

Almost all of it comes from Nordstrom's own open engineering and product postings, which name systems far more directly than any press release does. The rest comes from public filings around the take private in May 2025 and from trade coverage of the supplier RFID mandate and the west coast automation work.

I have no inside information and I have never worked on a packaged ERP, so treat the directional arrows as hypotheses rather than as a roadmap. The one I would most like corrected is RMS.

Where I would look for leverage

These are patterns rather than proposals. I would expect at least a few of them to already be underway, and the ones that are not are probably not underway for a reason I cannot see from out here.

  1. Reconciliation as a product, not a month-end report where I would start

    Most retailers find ledger discrepancies at close, which means the break gets investigated weeks after the transaction that caused it. The alternative is transaction-level detection with alerting, so a bad feed surfaces the day it lands and the investigation starts while the context is still warm.

    Closest thing I have built: a stockout root cause engine at Amazon Fresh that moved attribution from correlation to causation, four times more accurate than what it replaced.

  2. An alert without an owner does not get fixed where I would start

    Exception routing tends to get built as a queue. Queues accumulate. What changes behaviour is a named accountable party per exception class, plus a daily report that makes the backlog visible to the person who can clear it.

    At Amazon Go, a daily error report paired with explicit SKU ownership moved procurability from 93 percent to 99 percent.

  3. Decommissioning as a funded workstream with its own metric where I would start

    Migrations stall in the last twenty percent because nobody is measured on turning the old system off. Making decommissioning a tracked deliverable with a cost number attached turns cleanup into value delivery, which also makes it fundable.

    Consolidated more than thirty internal tools into eight workflows for an eight country grocery operation, with full adoption and no interruption to the operation.

  4. An append only transaction log, with the ledger as a projection

    If every receipt, sale, markdown, transfer and adjustment is an immutable event, then reconciliation becomes a replay rather than an investigation, and recovering from a bad feed becomes rebuilding a projection rather than hand correcting balances. The phrase distributed ledger transaction validation in your postings suggests this is already the direction.

  5. The gap between counting units and valuing them

    RFID has largely solved knowing how many are on the shelf. It does not touch valuation. Markdown timing, returns to vendor, shrink classification and transfer costing all move dollars without moving units, and they are a large share of what makes a ledger disagree with the floor.

    Replaced an ordering algorithm that optimised for labour with no shrink constraint, reducing shrink by 9.15 percent, worth roughly 6.4 million dollars a year.

  6. Strangler fig, with the new platform running in shadow first

    Run the new inventory platform in parallel computing the same numbers against live traffic, compare continuously, and cut over per function once divergence falls under an agreed threshold. It turns a migration decision into an evidence threshold.

    Ran the same pattern at small scale by prototyping at a single fulfilment centre before proposing the full build.

  7. Catch bad data at ingress rather than at reconciliation

    Validating a feed at the boundary is cheaper than discovering the consequence three systems downstream. Predictive validation on inbound data is the version of this that scales, and it is the only one of the four AI applications in the posting that pays for itself immediately.

    Two API contract changes made my service the source of truth for assortment coverage. Errors down twenty percent, data four times fresher, bad assortments down forty percent.

  8. A common inventory language is a contract problem before it is a schema problem

    Canonical models tend to die in committee, because every team wants their fields in it. Consumer driven contracts with automated contract tests survive, because the model only has to satisfy the systems actually consuming it, and breakage is caught in a pipeline rather than in production.

  9. The systems question and the ownership question are the same question

    A store inventory ownership migration is an org change wearing a systems name. You cannot settle where inventory truth lives in the data without also settling who is accountable for it, and the posting is unusually candid that scope and ownership are still forming.

    Won ownership of a sourceability calculation from two peer organisations, then moved it from 88 to 92 percent within three months.

What I would most like to ask

In rough order of how much the answer would change my understanding of the job.

  1. What is the thing about Nordstrom's inventory systems that outsiders consistently get wrong?
  2. Does RMS stay as the stock ledger of record, or is it eventually replaced too?
  3. Is the strategy best of breed SaaS per domain with an integration and ledger layer built in house, or is there a single target for inventory financials?
  4. When the ledger is wrong today, who finds out first, and how long does it take?
  5. Is the common inventory language a data contract, a canonical model, or an agreement between teams?
  6. What is the real driver: accuracy across store, distribution centre and digital, month end reconciliation pain, or the cost of running the legacy stack?
  7. Where is the boundary of this role today, and who else believes they own part of it?
  8. What has been tried against this before, and why did it not stick?
