Agentic Platform in the Software Development Lifecycle — Charter Proposal

Over the past five years, AI in development has moved from autocomplete to agents that plan work, write code, review it, test it, and open a pull request for a human to approve. This proposal traces that shift across the software development lifecycle: what it has genuinely changed, what remains unsolved, and where an internal agentic platform like ES365 can focus next.

A note on where this comes from. I prepared this while getting ready to meet the ES365 team. It draws on public research, company engineering blogs, and my own hands-on work with these tools. The people building engineering systems inside Microsoft see things I cannot from the outside, so I am offering this as a starting point for a conversation and expect to revise it as I learn what your team already knows.

Chapter 1

Five years, five waves

2021–2022, autocomplete. GitHub Copilot

GitHub Copilot suggested the next line of code inside a single file. It was a helpful convenience that a developer could accept or ignore.

2022–2023, chat. ChatGPT (OpenAI)Copilot Chat

ChatGPT and Copilot Chat let developers ask questions in plain language. A developer could paste an error and get an explanation without leaving the editor.

2023–2025, agents in the editor. CursorCopilot agent mode

Cursor and Copilot's agent mode began planning changes, editing across multiple files, and running commands while the developer supervised in real time.

2025, delegation. Claude CodeOpenAI CodexJules

Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Google Jules, and GitHub's coding agent now accept a ticket, work in the background, and return a pull request for a human to review. The unit of work became a task rather than a keystroke.

2026, platforms and control planes.

The current frontier is turning individual agents into an organizational capability. Microsoft's Agent Framework reached general availability in April 2026, and internal teams like ES365 are wrapping orchestration, governance, and a marketplace around agents so thousands of developers can use them safely. This is the wave this charter is about.

Chapter 2

Coding was only the beachhead

A typical delivery pipeline moves through planning, design, coding, review, testing, build, deployment, operations, migration, and documentation. AI tooling has reached all of these stages, and the honest assessment is that it has helped some far more than others.

  • Where the wins are measured: test generation, large mechanical migrations, and first-pass code review. Airbnb converted about 3,500 test files in six weeks, a project it had estimated at a year and a half. Google reports that 80 percent of code changes in its internal migrations are now AI-authored. First-pass review that once waited hours now arrives in seconds.
  • Where AI drafts and humans decide: planning, coding itself, build triage, incident response, and documentation. Planning agents decompose epics but do not decide what is worth building. Incident tools summarize an outage in minutes, but their root-cause guesses still need to be checked. Coding help is uneven, with large gains on unfamiliar or boilerplate work and weaker results for experts inside a mature codebase.
  • Where the problems remain open: architectural tradeoffs that depend on organizational context, deployment and configuration safety where a wrong change has a real blast radius, and trusting an agent's output without a person re-verifying it.

The stages in the first group share one property: a machine can check the result against tests.

Chapter 3

Perceived speed and measured speed diverge

Developers using these tools report feeling much faster. The measured results are less clear, and the gap between the two is where the interesting product problems sit.

The METR trial · 2025
+20%how much faster developers believed they were
−19%how much slower they actually were, measured

In a randomized trial, experienced engineers working in codebases they knew well were 19 percent slower with AI assistance, while estimating afterward that the tools had made them about 20 percent faster. This is why self-reported speedup cannot be used as the measure of success. (METR)

Google's DORA research found a matching pattern at the organizational level. As AI adoption rose, teams pushed more code through the pipeline while delivery stability declined. Larger batches shipped, and more of them broke. (DORA 2025)

What it means for a platform: generation improved faster than verification, so the bottleneck moved downstream into review, testing, and trust. A platform that speeds up writing without speeding up verification simply moves the pile of work to the next gate. The whole pipeline has to be addressed together.

Chapter 4

What the leaders have published

The companies reporting real numbers follow a common pattern: narrow agents pointed at verifiable work, with humans reviewing whatever lands.

Google

80 percent of code changes in landed internal migrations are AI-authored, with every change human-reviewed, and engineers spending roughly half the time they used to. (Google Research)

Amazon & Airbnb

Amazon reports that its Java-upgrade agent saved the equivalent of 4,500 developer-years and about 260 million dollars in annualized efficiency. Airbnb migrated roughly 3,500 test files in six weeks against an estimate of a year and a half. (AWS, Airbnb)

Stripe & Meta

Stripe's internal agents merge about 1,300 pull requests a week, started from a Slack message or a ticket. Meta publishes its filters as openly as its wins: most generated tests are discarded, and only the ones that provably add coverage are kept. (Stripe, Meta)

Spotify & Backstage

Spotify's internal developer platform, built on Backstage, the most widely used internal-developer-platform framework, now exposes plugins as tools any agent can discover, with the platform brokering permissions. The developer platform is becoming the control plane for agents, which is the model ES365 is pursuing inside Microsoft. (Spotify, Backstage)

Chapter 5

Why the next gains come from a platform

Individual tools brought the industry this far. But thousands of developers each wiring up their own agents produces duplicated effort, ungoverned access to code and credentials, and no consistent way to tell whether any of it is working.

The answer resembles the internal developer platforms that brought order to cloud infrastructure, now extended to agents:

  • Orchestration, so agents compose and reuse rather than reinvent.
  • Golden paths, so the well-instrumented route is also the easiest one.
  • A curated marketplace, so teams share agents instead of rebuilding them.
  • Governance, so an agent's access is brokered, scoped, and audited.

This is the ground ES365 covers, from source control through build, validation, and deployment. Measurement belongs here too. Acceptance rate flatters the tool. The figures that matter are outcomes: lead time, change failure rate, and where engineering hours actually go. Instrument the platform once, and every team gets trustworthy numbers.

Chapter 6

How I would approach the product role

Start from the developer's problem. The work begins with listening. I would sit with the developers and codebase owners who use these tools, learn where their day actually stalls, and pair that with a clear view of where the technology is heading. Humility about their pain points and conviction about the direction are both required, and neither substitutes for the other.

Set the goals and define success. I would set explicit objectives for the space and define how success is measured before building, using outcome metrics rather than acceptance rate: lead time, change failure rate, and the share of engineering time spent on new work. Agreeing on the scoreboard early is what keeps a platform honest as it scales.

The hardest part is alignment. The largest share of this job is not building. It is combining strong and often competing ideas from across Microsoft into one vision that developer teams will actually adopt. Azure's customer-facing teams, internal DevOps groups, GitHub, DevDiv, and individual product orgs all arrive with their own approaches, some complementary and some in tension. Much of the role is defending and evangelizing a shared charter so the company converges rather than fragments.

Then execute, measure, and iterate. With the vision agreed, the work becomes tactical: sequence the roadmap, ship in increments, watch the outcome metrics, and adjust. Move from early adopters to broad rollout along golden paths, and keep the feedback loop with developers tight enough that the platform keeps pace with what they actually need.

Chapter 7

Three Working Hypotheses

Hypothesis one

Make the safe path the fast path. Adoption follows convenience. If the governed, instrumented route is also the easiest one, mandates become unnecessary. I saw this directly when I consolidated more than 30 internal tools into 8 workflows at Amazon: adoption reached 100 percent because the new path was simply better to use, and usability scores rose 78 percent.

Hypothesis two

Measure outcomes on a balanced scorecard. Pair throughput with stability so a gain on one cannot quietly become a loss on the other, and track the gap between perceived and actual speedup, because the METR result shows it is real. Deciding what success looks like before shipping is what keeps a platform pointed at outcomes instead of activity.

Hypothesis three

Curate more than you build. The ecosystem will produce more agent capability than any single team can. The platform's leverage is discovery, quality bars, and trust, so a developer can adopt a shared agent with the same confidence as a standard library.

And the questions I would ask first: where does agent output actually stall today, in review, in CI, or in trust? Which workflows have the strongest verification, making them the best candidates for delegation? What does the cost curve look like at full scale, and where do smaller, cheaper models fit? The answers live inside ES365, with the team and the telemetry.
