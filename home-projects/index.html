How designers combine plants

🌱Right plant, right place

Match each plant to the sun, water and soil it will actually get. A beautiful combination of plants that hate the spot will still fail, so this filter comes first.

🌲Layering

Stack plants by height: canopy tree, smaller understory tree, shrubs, perennials and ferns, then groundcover, with vines climbing between. It gives the eye somewhere to travel and copies how a PNW forest edge is built.

📅Four-season interest

Plan a relay: spring and summer flowers, fall leaf color, then winter structure from evergreens, bark, berries and seed heads. Something should look good every month, not just in May.

🎨Color strategy

Neighboring colors (blue, purple, pink) feel calm; opposites (purple with yellow, blue with orange) pop. Repeating one color across several plants ties a bed together, and green foliage is the backbone that makes flowers read.

🍃Texture & form contrast

Put fine, feathery leaves next to big, bold ones so both stand out, and mix shapes: something upright, something mounding, something that spills. Container growers call it "thriller, filler, spiller".

🔁Repetition & drifts

Plant in groups of 3, 5 or 7 instead of one of everything, and repeat the same plant in a few spots. Repetition creates rhythm and looks intentional; odd numbers look natural.

📏Scale & spacing

Space plants for the size they'll reach in 5–10 years, not the size in the pot. A young bed looks sparse; fill the gaps with mulch or annuals rather than crowding.

🤝Plant communities & guilds

Some plants naturally grow together, like Douglas-fir, salal, Oregon grape and sword fern. An edible guild works the same way: a fruit tree with berry shrubs, herbs and an edible groundcover beneath.

🐝Ecological function

Keep something in flower from early spring into fall so pollinators always have food, and add berries, seeds and dense shrubs for birds. Natives usually do this best.

🧤Maintenance matching

Group plants that want the same care: thirsty with thirsty, drought-tolerant with drought-tolerant. It saves water and keeps one plant from being loved to death next to another.

Where These Came From

The library was built from Pacific Northwest catalogs and references. Claudette wrote the descriptions and notes; the 1–5 scores for cost, availability, popularity and rarity are educated estimates for Seattle-area nurseries, not quotes.

  • Great Plant Picks: a juried list of plants proven in maritime Northwest gardens, run by the Elisabeth C. Miller Botanical Garden in Seattle. This was the main source for the catalog picks.
  • King County Native Plant Guide: which plants are truly local natives, and how they behave here.
  • Oregon State Landscape Plants and NC State Extension Plant Toolbox: size, growing conditions and care details.
  • Xera Plants (Portland) and Burnt Ridge Nursery (Onalaska, WA): Northwest nursery catalogs, used for drought-tolerant picks and fruit trees.
  • Wikimedia Commons: every photo is freely licensed. Click any photo to see its photographer and license.
